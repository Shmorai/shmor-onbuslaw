Key rules at a glance

Some of the most useful rules for buying and selling online. Each one links to a full guide with the conditions and exceptions.

All consumer rights →
UK & EU

14-day cancellation

Most goods and services bought online from a business can be cancelled within 14 days without giving a reason. Some exceptions apply.

Cancellation rights →UK

Section 75 protection

Paid for something costing over £100 and up to £30,000 with a credit card? The card provider can be equally liable if the trader breaches the contract.

How Section 75 works →US

60 days to dispute

Under the Fair Credit Billing Act, write to your card issuer within 60 days of the statement that shows a billing error.

Disputing a charge →UK & EU

Faulty goods

Goods must match their description and be of satisfactory quality. In the UK, you can reject faulty goods for a full refund within 30 days.

Faulty goods rights →UK

Scam reimbursement

Since 7 October 2024, UK payment firms must reimburse most victims of bank transfer (APP) scams, up to £85,000 per claim.

Getting money back →EU

Withdrawal button

Since 19 June 2026, EU online traders must offer a clearly labelled “withdraw from contract” function during the cancellation period.

What changed →Brands

® means registered

Use ® only for a registered trademark. Claiming registration falsely is a criminal offence in the UK and can be misleading elsewhere.

™ vs ® →Creators

Label ads upfront

Paid posts, gifts and affiliate links must be clearly disclosed, for example with “#ad”, under FTC, UK and EU rules.

Sorting out a problem with an online purchase

  1. Know your rights

    Find the rule that fits: cancellation, faulty goods, non-delivery or a card dispute. Note any deadlines.

  2. Put it in writing

    Contact the seller, explain what went wrong, say what you want and give a reasonable deadline. Keep copies.

  3. Escalate if needed

    Use your card provider, an ADR scheme or ombudsman, a regulator or small claims court. See how to escalate.

Online law in plain English

Buying, selling and creating online is governed by a patchwork of consumer law, payment rules, data protection, advertising codes and intellectual property law. OnBusLaw turns that patchwork into practical guides: what the rule is, who it protects, the deadlines that matter and where to go next.

Every page covers the United States (mainly federal law, with state differences flagged), the United Kingdom and the European Union, because the answer to “can I get my money back?” or “do I need to disclose this link?” often depends on where you, and the business, are.
